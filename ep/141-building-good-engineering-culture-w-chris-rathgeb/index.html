building good engineering culture w/ chris rathgeb

guest: chris rathgeb, cto at curemit

  • build engineering culture by defining clear company values first, then hire people who align with them
  • write explicit, detailed definitions of success for each role so people are never blindsided on performance
  • hold weekly one on ones and do deeper quarterly reviews that revisit values and role fit
  • normalize code review from day one by pairing new engineers with a buddy and having them review pull requests early
  • when job hunting, ask how the company actually works day to day and how early engineers get involved in planning

questions this episode answers

what does chris rathgeb say about defining success for engineering roles?

on guidance counselor 2.0, chris rathgeb says companies must write explicit, detailed definitions of success across 11 different points including team engagement, planning and estimation, issue management, code quality, and progress tracking. he puts all these success criteria directly into job descriptions so candidates know exactly what the role requires. this prevents employees from being blindsided later when they thought things were going well but are suddenly let go.

how does chris rathgeb run performance reviews at curemit?

on guidance counselor 2.0, chris rathgeb says one-on-ones must happen weekly, not quarterly or annually, covering all 11 success areas so feedback is never a surprise. every direct report gets at least an hour per week, and each quarter they do a deeper 90 to 120 minute session revisiting company values and running a people analyzer assessment. the people analyzer asks three questions: do you get the job, do you want it, and do you have the capacity to succeed at it.

what does chris rathgeb recommend for reducing toxic code review culture?

on guidance counselor 2.0, chris rathgeb says to normalize code review from day one by pairing new engineers with a buddy and having them review all pull requests for the first 30 days. he requires that anyone who leaves critical feedback must invest time helping the engineer actually implement the changes, not just send and forget. this makes reviewers think twice before demanding half-day rewrites and eliminates the chest-pounding ego dynamic that makes engineers afraid to submit code.
