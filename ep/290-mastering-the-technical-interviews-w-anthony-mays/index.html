what anthony mays covers in this episode

anthony mays is a software engineer with 22 years in the industry, eight of them at google, where he worked on data visualization and analysis. he left to help more people get into tech, especially underrepresented folks, through one-on-one coaching with morgan latimer consulting and by working with companies on how they find and hire talent. this was his second time on guidance counselor 2.0, and the conversation centers on his course, mastering technical interviews, and the mindset behind it.

the problem usually isn't the code

anthony built the course after years of running mock interviews. what he kept seeing: most candidates didn't struggle with algorithms, data structures or writing code. the answers he expected were often simple. where people went wrong was not having a repeatable problem-solving strategy, not asking for the information they needed, and treating every interview like a brand new thing. he says he had the same struggle early on, and that wanting to be undeniable as a black man in tech pushed him to get intentional about it.

the course is about three hours of short three to five minute sections, and buying it gets you into a discord community where anthony answers questions directly. he talks about community as a tribe: people who share your values and help each other, not an email list.

know where the finish line is

taylor compares interviewing to a marathon, and anthony takes it further. you can't run a marathon without knowing where the finish line is, and every company has its own route. a course in la is different from one in boston, and an interview loop at one company is different from the next. so for each company and each role, figure out what success looks like there before you start running.

the interview is not about you

this is the line anthony repeats on purpose. companies care about their own problems and their bottom line, so like a good salesperson you have to show the company and the interviewer what's in it for them. one question he almost never hears from candidates: what are the top three problems you're trying to solve? asking it gives you the opening to connect your past work to their needs.

resumes: results over tools

on tailoring, anthony says shotgunning one resume to 100 companies is why people get five callbacks. use the words the job description uses, without lying, because a recruiter may spend only seconds on your resume. taylor shares his approach of a bullet point summary at the top that mirrors the role's stack, and anthony agrees, with one push: it's not about the tools. answer at least one of four questions in the first half of page one. how do you make the company money, save money, save time or drive efficiency?

ai in the job search

anthony sees ai and machine learning as the biggest shift he's watched since mobile and the internet. his practical uses: have chat gpt reword your resume against a job description, research a company's problems before a recruiter screen, and practice behavioral answers, which he calls an open book test.

his advice to his younger self: take informed risks. he once threw away his own google application in college because he didn't think they'd hire someone like him.

questions this episode answers

what does anthony mays say about what job seekers should focus on instead of just listing tools and technologies?

on guidance counselor 2.0, anthony mays says candidates should stop focusing only on the tools and technologies they know. instead, they need to show how they make money, save money, save time, or drive efficiency for the company. listing skills without demonstrating results is not enough to get hired.

what does anthony mays say about how candidates should think about the interview process?

on guidance counselor 2.0, anthony mays says the interview is not about you, it is about what you can do for the company. he explains that candidates need to show the company and the interviewer what is in it for them, not just expect their skills to shine through. treat interviewing like a marathon where you first need to know where the finish line is for each specific company and role.
