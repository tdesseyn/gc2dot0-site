mastering the technical interviews w/ anthony mays

guest: anthony mays, software engineer and interview coach, creator of mastering technical interviews

who anthony mays is

anthony mays is a software engineer with 22 years in the industry, eight of them at google, where he worked on data visualization and analysis. he left google during black history month to widen his impact: helping people get into tech, especially underrepresented folks, through one-on-one coaching with morgan latimer consulting, and working with companies on how they find and hire talent. he's also an advisor to karat's brilliant black minds program. this was his second time on guidance counselor 2.0, and the conversation centers on his course, mastering technical interviews, and the mindset behind it.

key takeaways

1. most candidates don't fail on the code

anthony built the course after years of running mock interviews. what he kept seeing: clients didn't struggle with algorithms, data structures or writing code. when he showed them the answer he expected, it usually wasn't complicated. where people went wrong was not having a repeatable, well-defined problem-solving strategy, not asking for the information they needed, and treating every interview like a brand new thing. he says that was his own struggle early on, and that wanting to be undeniable as a black man in tech pushed him to get intentional about it.

2. know where the finish line is

taylor compares interviewing to a marathon, and anthony takes it further. you can't run a marathon without knowing where the finish line is, and every course is different. la isn't boston. each company has its own process, its own route and its own definition of success, so for each company and each role, figure out what success looks like there before you start running.

3. the interview is not about you

this is the line anthony repeats on purpose. companies care about their own problems and their bottom line. like any good salesperson, you have to show the company and the interviewer what's in it for them. we don't buy products to make companies rich. we buy them because they help us do something we couldn't do without them.

4. ask the question almost nobody asks

when candidates get time to ask questions, anthony says almost nobody asks: what are the top three most important problems you're trying to solve? it's simple, and it gives you the opening to say you've solved something like that before, and how much money or time it saved.

5. tailor the resume, and lead with results

shotgunning one resume to 100 companies is how people end up with five callbacks. use the words the job description uses, without lying, because a recruiter may give your resume about seven seconds. taylor shares his approach, a bullet point summary at the top that mirrors the role's stack, and anthony agrees with one push: it's not about the tools. in the first half of page one, answer at least one of four questions. how do you make the company money, save money, save time or drive efficiency?

6. use ai as a prep partner

anthony calls ai and machine learning the biggest shift he's seen since mobile and the internet itself. his practical uses: have chat gpt reword your resume against a job description, research a company's problems before a recruiter screen, and practice behavioral answers. he calls behavioral interviews an open book test, since we all know roughly what the questions will be.

7. take informed risks

his advice to his younger self: take risks, informed ones. in college he threw away his own google application because he didn't think they'd hire someone like him. he says he was too afraid of failure to be successful.

what to do this week

  • pick one company you're interviewing with and write down what the finish line looks like there: the stages, who you'll meet, and what a strong yes looks like.
  • rewrite the top half of page one of your resume so it answers at least one of anthony's four questions with a real result.
  • for every open application, swap your summary bullets to use the job description's own words for skills you actually have.
  • add "what are the top three problems you're trying to solve?" to your questions list and plan which past project you'd connect to each likely answer.
  • paste one job description and your resume into an ai tool, ask for a reworded version, then edit it so every line is still true.
