ep 288 · nov 1, 2023

interviewing and recruiting w/todd libby

guest: todd libby, web developer and accessibility specialist

  • double check candidate names before reaching out since misspelling them kills credibility fast
  • read the candidate's profile closely so your outreach matches their actual title and skills, not unrelated roles
  • share interviewer names, interview format, and number of attendees ahead of time so candidates aren't surprised
  • build accessibility into a project from the start rather than bolting it on at the end to save time and stress
  • it is fine to say you don't know an answer in an interview as long as you explain how you would find it

questions this episode answers

what does todd libby say recruiters get wrong when reaching out to candidates?

on guidance counselor 2.0, todd libby says recruiters often misspell candidate names, which immediately kills credibility. he also says they frequently do not read profiles carefully and reach out about roles that do not match his actual title or skills, such as messaging him about data entry when he specializes in accessibility and developer experience. todd emphasizes that getting the name right and matching the role to his actual expertise are the bare minimum.

what interview prep information does todd libby want recruiters to share ahead of time?

on guidance counselor 2.0, todd libby says recruiters should tell candidates exactly who will be on the interview call, how many people will attend, and the interview format or style. he shares that he has been surprised by five or sixteen people showing up when he expected one person, which feels like an ambush. todd also wants to know if the interview will be rapid fire technical questions or another specific format so he is not caught off guard.

what does todd libby recommend saying when you don't know an interview answer?

on guidance counselor 2.0, todd libby says it is okay to say you don't know an answer in an interview as long as you explain how you would find it. he suggests saying you can look it up, ask a teammate for help, or consult someone who knows the answer. todd warns that stopping at just saying you don't know is a mistake, and that explaining your problem-solving approach shows resourcefulness instead of ending the conversation.
