ep 102 · jan 17, 2022

w/alishah novin, director of engineering

guest: alishah novin, director of engineering at salero commerce

  • skip take home coding tests and instead ask candidates to walk through a project they actually built
  • in interviews ask what you were trying to build, what went wrong, and what you would change next time
  • resumes still matter so put your programming languages and tools at the top followed by quantifiable experience
  • avoid vague skill ratings like javascript four out of five since reviewers have no idea what your scale means
  • include non tech jobs like customer service or serving on your resume if the skills are relevant to the role

who alishah novin is

alishah novin is a director of engineering at celero commerce, a fintech company, where he led a large agile transformation. he's been in tech for close to 20 years and grew up coding, but found he loves management even more, because it swaps code problems for people and process problems. he and taylor trade notes often: alishah is the hiring manager, taylor the recruiter. this episode started after a live show on technical assessments, and it's a hiring manager's honest take on assessments and resumes, with a lot of live questions from listeners.

key takeaways

1. coding tests rarely predicted who was great

alishah has whiteboarded, faced odd brain teasers, and given assessments himself. looking back, how someone did on the assessment didn't tie to how good a teammate they became. his analogy: you don't ask a carpenter to build you a chair on the spot. you look at their portfolio. he thinks companies lean on tests because they don't know another way to assess skill, and because code shows exactly how your brain works, being judged on it under stress feels personal and rarely brings out someone's best.

2. take-homes teach you less than you think

a listener asked what a take-home offers that other evaluations don't. alishah's blunt answer: he questions how many teams review them well. if nobody sits down with the candidate and says walk me through your code, you mostly learn whether it runs and which approach they picked. you miss the thinking behind it. he's never found a candidate who did a take-home and turned out exceptionally better than someone who didn't, and he thinks filtering this way loses great people.

3. option three: walk me through something you built

his favorite format is a candidate bringing a project they built on their own and talking him through it. what were you trying to build, what problem were you solving, what did you run into, what did you do wrong, what would you change? the key, he says, isn't measuring people equally, it's measuring fairly. he only pulls out a whiteboard question when he likes someone's attitude but is on the fence about their skills, as a way to give them another shot.

4. data structure and algorithm questions test memory, not skill

he says, crediting someone he respects named james, that classic sorting algorithms came out of years of academic research. asking for one in ten minutes under stress means asking someone to out-think a phd or recite something memorized. the best version he experienced was an interviewer who helped along the way, which showed how they'd problem solve together. it comes down to the interviewer, not the format.

5. sign your resume with excellence

he shares a saying that changed his view of perfectionism: perfection is procrastination in disguise. what he wants is for people to sign their work with excellence, so anything you hand over looks like a final draft. on layout: languages and tools at the top, then experience made as quantifiable as possible. skip headshots and fancy formats, and never rate yourself, like javascript four out of five, because nobody knows what your five means.

6. count projects, not just years

taylor's summary format runs general to specific: total years, then years per language or framework, then degree. alishah adds that years don't always equal experience, so list how many projects you've done. right out of college with a physics degree, he took every small job he could find on craigslist and walked into his first interview with about 20 projects.

7. show your desire to learn in public

include non-tech jobs if relevant, since customer service or serving tables matters in customer facing coding roles. use readable, clickable links on a pdf resume. the quality he values most in juniors is a desire to learn, and one favorite hire kept a blog about what they were reading, building and struggling with.

what to do this week

  • pick one project you built and write a short paragraph on the problem, what went wrong and what you'd change. put it next to the code.
  • practice walking someone through that project out loud in five minutes.
  • move your languages and tools to the top of your resume and delete any self-ratings.
  • add a project count to your summary if your years undersell your experience.
  • if you hire, sit down with your next take-home candidate and ask them to walk you through their code.
