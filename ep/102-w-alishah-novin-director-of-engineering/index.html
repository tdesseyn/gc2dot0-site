what alishah novin covers in this episode

alishah novin is a director of engineering at a fintech company, where he led a large agile transformation. he's been in tech for close to 20 years and grew up coding, but says he found he loves management even more, because it swaps code problems for people and process problems. this episode is a hiring manager's honest take on technical assessments and resumes, with a lot of live questions from listeners.

why he skips take-home tests

a listener asked what value hiring managers actually get from a take-home assessment. alishah's blunt answer: he questions how many teams review them well. if nobody sits down with the candidate and says walk me through your code, you learn very little. a reviewer might compile it, check that it runs, and see whether the candidate took one approach or another, but you miss the thinking behind the solution. he says he's never found a candidate who did a take-home and turned out exceptionally better than someone who didn't, and that filtering this way loses a lot of great people.

option three: walk me through something you built

instead of a take-home or a live coding test, his favorite approach is having a candidate bring a project they built on their own and talk him through it. what were you trying to build, what problem were you solving, what did you run into, what did you do wrong, and what would you change next time? he says you can learn a lot from those questions without asking someone to reverse a string. the only time he pulls out a whiteboard question now is when he really likes a candidate's attitude but is on the fence about their skills, as a way to give them another shot.

sign your resume with excellence

yes, resumes still matter to him. he shares a saying that changed how he thinks about perfectionism: perfection is procrastination in disguise. what he wants instead is for people to sign their work with excellence, meaning everything you hand over should look like a final draft, not a rough one. he can tell who spent time on their resume.

his layout advice: put your programming languages and tools at the top where they're most visible, then your experience, made as quantifiable as possible. skip the headshots and fancy formats. and don't rate yourself, like javascript four out of five, because nobody knows what your five means. taylor adds his summary format, general to specific: total years of engineering, then years in each language or framework, then your degree. alishah's add-on is that years don't always equal experience, so counting projects can say more.

non-tech jobs and what he looks for in juniors

should you include non-tech jobs? if they're relevant, yes. some coding roles are customer facing, and experience as a server or in customer service is a real skill set. he also prefers readable, clickable links on a pdf resume.

the quality he values most in a junior developer is a desire to learn, and being able to show it. one of his favorite hires kept a blog about what they were reading and building, including what they were struggling with. putting that kind of work in public, on a portfolio or github, tells him a lot about a person.

questions this episode answers

what does alishah novin recommend instead of take-home coding tests?

on guidance counselor 2.0, alishah novin recommends asking candidates to walk through a project they actually built rather than giving take-home assessments. he suggests asking what they were trying to build, what problems they ran into, and what they would change if they did it again. he finds this conversational approach reveals more about how someone thinks and works than watching them complete an artificial coding challenge.

how should developers structure their resume according to alishah novin?

on guidance counselor 2.0, alishah novin says to put your programming languages and tools at the top of your resume, followed by quantifiable experience. he recommends avoiding vague skill ratings like javascript four out of five because reviewers have no idea what your scale means. in the summary, start general and get specific, like ten years overall experience, seven in development, three in a specific technology, so hiring managers immediately know what you do.

should developers include non-tech jobs on their resume?

on guidance counselor 2.0, alishah novin says to include non-tech jobs like customer service or serving if the skills are relevant to the coding role. he explains that some coding positions are customer facing, so experience as a waiter or in customer service provides valuable transferable skills regardless of what others might think.
