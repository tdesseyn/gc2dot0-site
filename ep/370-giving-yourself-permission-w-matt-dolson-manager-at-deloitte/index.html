giving yourself permission w/ matt dolson, manager at deloitte

guest: matt dolson, manager, human capital at deloitte

  • look for ways to apprentice others or learn from someone with different experience, regardless of title or tenure
  • if your company lacks an apprenticeship or mentorship program, give yourself permission to start something small instead of waiting for approval
  • build in regular moments to celebrate wins on your team, no win is too small to acknowledge
  • leadership is about actions and skills anyone can show up with daily, not a title you wait to receive
  • before chasing a new role, get clear on your own values and who you are so you can tell if a culture fits

who matt dolson is

matt dolson is a manager in the human capital practice at deloitte. his work is the people side of large tech implementations and process redesigns: helping people understand what's changing and why, getting buy-in, and building the knowledge and skills they need to thrive in what comes next. he also does career coaching, and describes himself as a lifelong learner who counts a day as a success if he helped someone get closer to their potential. there were no set questions. taylor asked what was on matt's mind, and the conversation ran through three ideas, apprenticeship, permission and celebration, plus what holds people back in his coaching.

key takeaways

1. anyone can apprentice someone

matt uses apprenticeship to mean passing along your knowledge and real life experience so someone doesn't have to learn it the hard way. it isn't tied to title or tenure. someone two years in can apprentice a new hire, and someone ten years in can learn from a newer person who's now above them. his example: when a teammate volunteers to present, set up dry runs and give real time feedback, like counting filler words. without that voice, he says, people never really improve. he sees people hungry for that kind of insight, and notes it's harder in a hybrid world where you can't just grab a conference room.

2. apprenticeship is a culture and retention play

asked why companies need these programs, matt points to retention and culture. if work is purely transactional, people show up, check boxes and leave. most people want somewhere they can show up authentically and build relationships, and if people don't feel their best, you won't get their best. in a hybrid world, he adds, companies should worry about presenteeism: being there without giving all of yourself.

3. give yourself permission

if your company has no program, matt's answer is his second theme. we default to waiting for permission. if what you want to do is reasonable, moral and ethical, just start. he did that with matt's monday motivation, a weekly leadership email that turns something from his week, like finishing the trim in a bathroom, into a lesson about not skipping the final details. he started it in an organization of more than 400,000 people because he saw a need, not because someone said he could. no program in place? good, he says. you get to be the one who makes it happen.

4. no win is too small to celebrate

people yearn for celebration, and when one person pauses a meeting to recognize something, it ripples out. every friday his team has a 30 minute wellness block with no work talk: weekend plans, what they're excited about, how they took care of their well-being. only after that human moment do they hold a 30 minute work meeting with a start, stop, continue exercise. culture, he says, is the sum of individual actions.

5. leadership isn't a title

taylor's view is that you don't need a title to lead. matt, who studied this in grad school, separates the two: a leader has positional power because of the role, while leadership is a set of skills and actions anyone can bring every day. creating space, setting expectations, delegating and letting people own something all count. at his organization, he says, you're expected to perform at the next level consistently for about a year before a promotion, so nobody gets promoted into burnout.

6. know who you are, and don't make the next job the last job

in his coaching, the most common blocker is a lack of self-awareness, and he stresses it isn't people's fault, since adults are rarely given time for it. he suggests the enneagram or a forced rank of your top five values, so you can tell whether a culture fits. the other blocker is believing the next step has to be the last step. his reframe: what if it's just right for you right now?

7. perfection is the enemy of good enough

his advice to his younger self: there's rarely one right answer at work, just a spectrum of options. fail fast, fail forward, and bring a recommendation and a point of view instead of waiting for the perfect answer.

what to do this week

  • offer one teammate a dry run before their next presentation and give specific, real time feedback.
  • pick one small thing you've been waiting for permission to start, check that it's reasonable and ethical, and start it.
  • open your next team meeting by celebrating one win, however small.
  • write down your top five values in forced rank order and compare them to your current role.
  • if you're weighing a move, ask whether it's right for you right now, not forever.
