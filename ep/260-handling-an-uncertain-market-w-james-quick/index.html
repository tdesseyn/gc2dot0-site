ep 260 · aug 16, 2023

handling an uncertain market w/james quick

guest: james quick, developer, speaker, and content creator

  • when you get laid off, resist posting a generic update and instead lean on relationships you already built in your community
  • share your work consistently on twitter, discord, or a blog so people can verify your skills before you ever apply
  • treat content as proof for your resume claims, since anyone can watch your videos or read your posts and see if the skill is real
  • if a recruiter or contact does not respond, follow up with empathy a couple more times instead of sending canned repeated messages
  • ask your employer for time and resources to learn new things rather than assuming all growth has to happen on unpaid personal time

what james quick covers in this episode

james quick describes himself as a developer, speaker and teacher, with about ten years of experience across developer relations, developer advocacy, engineering and architecture. he was let go from his job in july 2022 and used it as the push to try full-time content creation, something he'd been scared to do. this episode was recorded about eight months into that, with layoffs all around, and it's about why community and content matter so much when the market turns.

what happened when he got laid off

after being let go, james posted a simple message on twitter: he'd been let go, didn't know his next steps, and people could send him a dm. over a night or two, he got about 100 dms. many weren't just we have an open role, but closer to we'd love to hire you. he's careful to say this is a privileged position most people don't have. but he says it came from years of creating content, being in the community and earning people's respect, so when he needed to lean on it, he could.

start by being active somewhere

when taylor asks where people should start, james says just be active somewhere. for him that's twitter, and he says even replying to others and joining conversations builds visibility. share your work constantly, on twitter, discord, your own blog or wherever developers are. now, when james goes to conferences, he already knows people from those conversations.

content as proof for your resume

james makes a strong case for content as validation. a resume is words on a page. if it says you're a good communicator, a company has no way to verify that before the interview. content is different. if you want to know whether he's a good communicator, he says, watch one of his 500 videos. if you want to know whether someone can write, read their blog. that proof helps you get the interview and gives you something real to talk about in it. a few listeners chimed in with their own wins, including an interview that came after a cto heard someone speak in a twitter space.

follow up, but with empathy

how many times should you follow up before it's spam? taylor says three. james says three is the max, and he'd probably stop at two. what bothers him are canned sequences that never stop. he once got a series of automated messages that escalated into a story about a pet dog being upset he hadn't replied. if you follow up, don't repeat the same message. acknowledge the person is busy, explain why talking to them matters to you, and accept that you won't hear back from everyone. taylor notes that recruiters often skip feedback because they feel bad, so asking directly and making it easy can get you an answer.

ask your employer to fund your learning

james also says your employer should support your learning: trainings, conferences, or an hour or two a week on something not directly tied to your job, even a speaking class. if you're doing side projects to learn, see how much of that time you can get from your full-time job instead of only your evenings.

his career advice centers on what he gets to do now: work on exactly what he loves, something he says most people don't think is an option.

questions this episode answers

what does james quick say about posting on social media after getting laid off?

on guidance counselor 2.0, james quick says that when he was laid off from planetscale, he posted a simple message on twitter saying he had been let go and asked if anyone was hiring. overnight he received 100 direct messages from people offering job opportunities or saying they would love to hire him. he warns this may sound insensitive because most people do not have that network, but it shows the power of building relationships and creating content before you need them.

how does james quick recommend developers start building a network?

on guidance counselor 2.0, james quick recommends just being active somewhere like twitter, discord, or a blog. he says you do not even need to create full posts at first; responding to others and participating in conversations counts. share your work consistently so people can see what you know, because content validates the words on your resume in a way employers can actually verify before an interview.

how many times should you follow up with someone who does not respond, according to james quick?

on guidance counselor 2.0, james quick says three messages is the maximum before it becomes spam. he recommends not repeating the same message; instead, show empathy by acknowledging the person is busy and explaining why talking to them matters to you. he also warns against canned sequences or staged messages because they are impersonal and annoying.
