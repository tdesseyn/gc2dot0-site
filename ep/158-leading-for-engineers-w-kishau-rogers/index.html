leading for engineers w/ kishau rogers

guest: kishau rogers, ceo at time study

  • define success metrics with your engineering team so everyone aligns on what matters and can work their own way
  • build rest into project planning because burnout shows up as slower, less creative work if you dont
  • create sandbox time so teams can tinker and break things without judgment before building the real thing
  • use transparent hiring steps and prep candidates on what to expect so interviews feel conversational, not an interrogation
  • in job interviews explain the impact of your work, not just the skills, including customer feedback and team context

what kishau rogers covers in this episode

kishau rogers is a computer scientist and tech entrepreneur, and the ceo of time study, a company that, in her words, studies time, with close to 100 hospitals around the us as customers at the time of this episode. she started in tech as a programmer almost 25 years earlier and founded her first company more than 17 years before this conversation. time study is her third. she describes her job simply: she wrangles complexity. this episode is about leading engineers well, building culture, and hiring in a way that treats candidates like people.

success metrics everyone agrees on

kishau makes a deliberate effort to define how success is measured and get the engineering team aligned on it. in team meetings, everyone leads with their metrics, which keeps things quick. once everyone agrees on what matters, people can work the way they want to work, and she doesn't pester engineers on weekends. she remembers pulling all-nighters as an engineer because the quiet hours were her most creative time, and she thinks engineers are more creative than they get credit for.

she looks at the usual things, like work completed, unplanned items and reopened tickets, and adds one more: customer feedback on big releases. that gives engineers something most don't have in an interview, a way to explain not just their skills but how their work affected the people using it.

build rest into the plan

kishau watches the rhythm of her team, the stretches of intense, productive coding and the points where people need rest. so they build rest into project planning. if you don't, she says, people won't rest anyway, and you'll see burnout as slower, less crisp work. listeners in the chat called it one of the rarest mindsets they'd heard.

make room for play

she also wants teams to have sandbox time: a safe place, on a regular schedule, to tinker and break things without being judged, especially for younger workers who learn by taking things apart. and before building something new, she asks teams to mock it up first, on a napkin, in excel or in a doc, without any code, and imagine the customer using it.

hiring without the interrogation

kishau starts by sharing what success looks like in the hiring process, so candidates can quickly tell whether the role is for them. every candidate gets a 30 minute intro call with her, even when someone else on her team is hiring, to introduce herself and the company and learn why they're looking. next is a situational interview with the relevant team member about what they'd do in the role, and candidates are told ahead of time what will be covered and which stories to have ready. for executive roles, there's a third step with investors or advisors. she sees no reason to set a candidate up to fail, and since the questions are about her company's own use cases, nobody can google the answers.

she also lets employees say openly when their long-term goal isn't at her company, like starting their own business, and helps them build toward it while they're there.

her advice for job seekers: don't just list what you know. explain the impact. who did your work help, who did you build it with, and how many customers used it? if you don't know, ask a former supervisor.

questions this episode answers

what does kishau rogers say about defining success metrics for engineering teams?

on guidance counselor 2.0, kishau rogers says she makes a deliberate effort to define how success is measured and align the entire engineering team around those metrics. everyone leads team meetings by going through their metrics, which keeps the team aligned quickly. this approach allows engineers to work the way they want to work, so she does not pester them on weekends because she understands engineering flow state. she also measures customer feedback as a success metric so engineers can build portfolios that explain not just their skills but the impact of their work on customers.
