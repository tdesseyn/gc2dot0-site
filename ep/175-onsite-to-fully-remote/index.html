ep 175 · oct 5, 2022

onsite to fully remote

guest: sarah, head of business development at vault athletics

  • test a four day work week with a trial period and survey employees to see if productivity actually holds up, not just morale
  • build in forced rest between work sprints since burnout drops output just like it does for athletes in training
  • replace office water cooler moments with small structured things like random small group chats or casual team channels for non work talk
  • give people a personal development fund and a remote work stipend so they can choose what actually helps them, instead of generic happy hours
  • let employees choose their schedule and location instead of forcing onsite days, since forced choices breed resistance and autonomy builds buy in

questions this episode answers

what does sarah say about implementing a four-day work week at a startup?

on guidance counselor 2.0, sarah from vault athletics says her company started summer fridays in july 2020 as a trial, then surveyed all employees and department heads. when 97 percent reported their work was not just the same but actually better, vault made the four-day week permanent. she explains productivity increased because forced rest prevents burnout, just like work-rest intervals in athletic training prevent fatigue.

how can remote companies build culture without forcing people back to the office, according to sarah?

on guidance counselor 2.0, sarah from vault athletics says small structured touchpoints work better than forcing office days. her company uses random three-person donut chats in slack that are opt-in and ban work talk, plus casual channels for recipes and tv recommendations where introverts can participate asynchronously. vault also offers a personal development fund and a remote work stipend so employees choose what actually helps them, instead of generic happy hours.

why does sarah say forcing hybrid schedules backfires even when employees liked being onsite?

on guidance counselor 2.0, sarah from vault athletics says she loved the office routine before the pandemic but would now resist being forced back onsite, even though she still enjoys occasional in-person coffee meetings. she explains that one of her friends at a big tech company would be happy coming in two days a week if she could choose which days, but hates it when the company mandates specific days. sarah says forced choices breed resistance because people need autonomy to feel bought in.
