ep 199 · dec 28, 2022

streaming, shotgunning, & twitter

guest: drew clemitz, front-end engineer

  • when you lose income fast, start building something publicly while you apply, it validates you to employers
  • a single honest tweet about hitting rock bottom can snowball into leads if you already have some network built
  • streaming your coding process in public forces you to think out loud, which builds the same muscle interviews need
  • mix tactics, build and stream during the day, then block out late hours to mass apply and cross post your project
  • find a real community like virtual coffee instead of just lurking, consistent small shows of effort compound over time

questions this episode answers

what does drew clemitz say about streaming your coding publicly during a job search?

on guidance counselor 2.0, drew clemitz says he streamed himself building a react project for six hours a day while job searching, and that body of work became 90 percent responsible for landing his first contract role. he explains that streaming forces you to verbalize your thought process, which builds the same communication muscle needed in technical interviews. drew also notes that if you can get comfortable streaming in public, interviews will feel easier by comparison.

how did drew clemitz balance building projects and mass applying during his job search?

on guidance counselor 2.0, drew clemitz says he would stream and build projects for six hours during the day, take time off for family in the afternoon, then return to his computer from 10 p.m. to midnight to mass apply to jobs. during that late-night block he would scour every job board he could find, cross-posting roles to his own job board project while also applying to them himself.

what does drew clemitz say about posting your job loss on twitter?

on guidance counselor 2.0, drew clemitz says he posted on twitter that he had just lost both sources of income with one child and another on the way, and that single vulnerable tweet was retweeted over 600 times and continued getting reshared two years later. he explains that he knew the tech twitter community was willing to help because he had previously seen developers respond generously to small technical questions, so he believed people would go even farther to help someone at rock bottom.
