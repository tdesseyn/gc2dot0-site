ep 67 · aug 13, 2021

what developers want

with taylor desseyn (solo)

  • offer developers both a leadership track and an individual contributor track so strong coders are not forced into managing people
  • let senior individual contributors own a specific product area and keep advancing without becoming a manager
  • build mentorship opportunities into career growth since many developers want to teach without taking on full leadership
  • give real affirmations and specific guidance instead of vague criticism to help retain your team
  • let leaders step back to an individual contributor role after burning out without treating it as a failure

questions this episode answers

what does taylor desseyn say about career tracks for developers who don't want to manage people?

in this guidance counselor 2.0 solo episode, taylor desseyn says organizations need both a leadership track and an individual contributor track so strong developers aren't forced into management. he references nate taylor from pluralsight describing how caterpillar let individual contributors rise to level 20 or 21 while owning specific products, like one engineer who owned all lighting systems. taylor argues companies plateau individual contributors while letting people managers keep advancing, but that shouldn't be the case.

how does taylor desseyn say developers should be able to mentor without leading?

in this guidance counselor 2.0 solo episode, taylor desseyn says many developers want to teach or mentor but not necessarily lead people, so companies should build mentorship into career advancement. he describes mentorship as diet leadership and a way to test people for management, noting he started at a company mentoring three people before moving into a manager role. he emphasizes that developers constantly tell him they want mentorship opportunities or to teach part-time at places like coding bootcamps.

what does taylor desseyn say about managers giving feedback to retain developers?

in this guidance counselor 2.0 solo episode, taylor desseyn says managers need to give real affirmations constantly, even for small wins, not vague criticism or trophies. he agrees with a commenter that managers hurt retention when they just say no or that's not what i want without giving actual guidance or explaining what was tried before. taylor says managers should acknowledge what someone wants to do, explain past attempts, and ask if they can approach the problem differently so the person feels validated.
