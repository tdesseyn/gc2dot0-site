what ryan carson covers in this episode

ryan carson is the founder of treehouse and the ceo of untangle, a two-person company that raised a small seed round and, in his words, is building something that would have taken 50 to 100 people. he calls himself an optimistic techno believer, not a doomer. this episode is a blunt look at what hiring looks like when ai agents do more of the work, and what job seekers, especially junior developers, should do about it.

every information worker becomes an agent manager

ryan's core claim: every information worker who's being displaced or not getting hired needs to become an agent manager. that's the job now. whether the work is marketing, engineering, finance or operations, if you can push agents to complete important work, that's valuable and you can get hired for it. he says the skills are the same ones it takes to manage people: communicate goals clearly, give clear feedback, and keep your systems organized.

how he manages agents

ryan separates personal assistants, the ones that help with your life admin, from the tools you use to produce work. for work, pick an agent harness, like claude code, codex, amp code or devin, which he uses day in and day out. he prefers agents that run in the cloud, so the work doesn't stop when you close your laptop. his goal is to manage 50 to 100 agents, far more than the 10 to 15 people a human manager can handle well.

agents need what a new hire needs: what's my job, how am i measured, how do i know i'm done, where is everything? that means agents.md files and skills, plus automations to check the work. his setup uses linear tickets as the single source of truth, and every 30 minutes an automation reads each ticket, opens the session working on it, and makes sure it's still moving. he admits it doesn't work perfectly, but it works.

show your agent work to get hired

taylor brings up a theme he's been posting about: giving teams room to spend on ai tools could become a way to attract and keep talent. ryan agrees that the more you can show you're using agents, the more hireable you are, because most people don't know how to do this yet. to a listener asking whether a $20 a month plan is enough, he says if you want employees to get real work done, give them at least a $200 a month plan.

what is a junior dev now?

ryan's definition: a junior dev is someone who hasn't shipped much production code. so the way out is to ship production code yourself. find a simple idea, figure out how to charge for it, build it, and get real paying users. his bar is to find 20 people in the world who will pay you some money for something you built, then do it again with a second small app over a year or two. he's direct that if you can't, he doesn't think you're hireable today. he says engineering teams are shrinking, often to one or two people per feature, and the only path he sees is being a highly competent agent operator who has shipped to production.
