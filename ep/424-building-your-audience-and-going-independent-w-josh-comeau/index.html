building your audience and going independent w/ josh comeau

guest: josh comeau, software developer and creator of online courses at joshwcomeau.com

  • treat your time like a limited resource and spend it on work you actually care about
  • start a blog just to document how you solved problems so you do not relearn them later
  • build an audience and teaching experience before you try to create a paid course
  • when reviewing feedback look for patterns across many people rather than reacting to one comment
  • play to your natural strengths, like building and sharing work online, instead of forcing networking styles that do not fit you

questions this episode answers

what does josh comeau say about starting a blog as a developer?

on guidance counselor 2.0, josh comeau says he started blogging in 2014 mainly to document solutions to problems so he wouldn't have to relearn them later. he wrote posts on medium that initially got only 200 views, but the practice taught him how to blog and helped him build a reputation over time. he emphasizes that blogging doesn't need to be influencer-level content; simply documenting your problem-solving process creates a body of work that shows you know what you're talking about.

how does josh comeau recommend preparing before creating a paid course?

on guidance counselor 2.0, josh comeau says creating the course is not step one but step seven. the two necessary ingredients are building an audience through blogging and getting experience teaching in person or in real time. he spent years teaching at a local coding bootcamp on evenings and weekends, which helped him learn how to teach and understand where students are so he could meet them there. without these steps, you'll either have no audience to buy the course or the content won't meet students at their actual level.

what does josh comeau say about handling feedback on courses or projects?

on guidance counselor 2.0, josh comeau says he has collected over ten thousand pieces of feedback on his courses and looks for patterns across many people rather than reacting to individual comments. he waits a few months to let feedback accumulate, then sorts it by lesson and only acts if he sees the same issue mentioned more than once. he explains that if only one person out of fifteen thousand mentions something, it's probably their personal idiosyncrasy rather than a real problem with the material.
