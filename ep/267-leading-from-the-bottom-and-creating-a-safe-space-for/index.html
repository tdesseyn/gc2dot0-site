ep 267 · sep 1, 2023

leading from the bottom and creating a safe space for feedback

guest: ben, cto of a healthcare company building emr tech for hospice and home care

  • let your team run their plan even if you think yours is better, since ownership and buy-in beat a forced plan
  • treat bad news from your team as a sign of trust and safety, then ask what they need from you to fix it
  • if employees might not be telling you the truth, get 360 feedback from their peers since someone usually has better visibility than you do
  • when hiring, prioritize aptitude and attitude over exact tech stack experience since good people ramp up fast with solid onboarding
  • move fast in the hiring process and give candidates feedback quickly instead of making them wait weeks to hear back

questions this episode answers

what does ben say about letting your team execute their plan instead of yours?

on guidance counselor 2.0, ben, a cto at a healthcare company, says if his team proposes a plan that he rates a seven or eight when his own is a nine or ten, he will let them execute theirs 100 times out of 100. he explains that people own their plan and feel more accountable when it's theirs, and executing their own plan builds confidence. forcing a plan down means people won't be as excited about it.

how should a manager react when a team member brings bad news, according to ben?

on guidance counselor 2.0, ben, a cto at a healthcare company, says when someone brings you bad news it means you've established trust and they feel safe being authentic. he recommends celebrating it by saying thank you first, then asking what they need you to do about it, whether that's getting involved, listening or unblocking something. if people never bring bad news to you, bad things are still happening but you're not included in the conversation.

what does ben recommend for hiring managers in tech right now?

on guidance counselor 2.0, ben, a cto at a healthcare company, says move quickly through the hiring process, going from monday to friday with a hire instead of making people wait weeks. give candidates feedback as soon as you know it, especially if they did an interview. don't burden people with complicated take-home assignments unless you compensate them, and hire for aptitude and attitude instead of exact tech stack experience since good people ramp up fast with solid onboarding.
