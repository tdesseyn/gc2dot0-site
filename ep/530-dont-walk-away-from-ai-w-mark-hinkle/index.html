ep 530 · sep 28, 2026

don't walk away from ai w/ mark hinkle

guest: mark hinkle, founder of peripety labs and organizer of all things ai

  • treat ai like a tool you actually drive, not a ferrari parked in the garage you never take out
  • look for the easy wins first, like cutting down time spent in email or copy pasting between tools
  • use one model to plan and a different model to check its work so mistakes get caught earlier
  • ask yourself not just what ai can do but what you should actually hand off to it
  • keep showing up to in person events since trust and empathy still get built face to face, not through a screen

what mark hinkle covers in this episode

mark hinkle has about thirty years as a tech executive. he was an executive at cloud.com, ran the node.js foundation as executive director, was vp of marketing at the linux foundation, and co-founded a venture backed startup. four or five years ago he started peripety labs, named for the moment the hero realizes he's wrong, and began running ai meetups in north carolina's triangle. he later teamed up with todd lewis, founder of all things open, to start all things ai, which has sold out two conferences. this episode is his case for why you shouldn't walk away from ai, and how to start using it without getting overwhelmed.

a ferrari parked in the garage

mark compares having ai and barely using it to owning a ferrari you only drive up and down your street. at the same time, just because you have a hammer doesn't make everything a nail. he points to two easy places to start. an average knowledge worker spends about 28 percent of their time in email, and around 50 percent cutting and pasting between systems: pulling your calendar, email and meeting transcripts together just to write a report. that kind of information shuffling is low hanging fruit. he's moved a lot of his own email work to chatting with his gmail instead of sorting it through thirty years of folders.

not can, but should

when taylor says the new unlock is training your brain to go ai first for everything, mark reframes it: the question isn't whether ai can do something, it's whether it should. he could ride a unicycle to the gym, but he'd rather drive the truck. the test is whether handing something off makes you better, faster or stronger.

the bigger question he asks people is what they could do if they didn't have to do the busywork. for him, it's one more webinar that helps people, or building software that would help his team, sponsors and attendees at all things ai.

two models are better than one

on the more technical side, mark uses a higher-end model for planning, because planning matters most, then drops to a cheaper model for the work, and has models check each other's work so mistakes get caught. he says you don't need to be a coder to connect them. he asked claude how to get it talking to chatgpt, and it walked him through it.

why in-person still matters

for all the talk about automating work, mark is firm that you'll lose touch without real people around you. the relationships he built in the analog world are what let him survive in the digital one. two things he thinks agents won't replace are empathy and trust. it's hard to be empathetic toward an agent, and online you're often wondering whether you're even talking to a real person. in a sea of fake resumes and ai slop, he says going to a meetup and shaking someone's hand could do more for your career than mass applying everywhere.

his career advice: be a continuous learner. his grandfather was a bank vice president his whole career with three tools: an adding machine, a phone and green ledger paper. today, he says, nobody can get by with three tools.

questions this episode answers

what does mark hinkle say about starting with ai at work?

on guidance counselor 2.0, mark hinkle says knowledge workers spend 28 percent of their time in email and 50 percent cutting and pasting between systems, so those are easy places to start. he suggests looking at what you do every day and asking whether you add any value to that task. if the answer is no, that task is a good candidate for ai.

what does mark hinkle say about meeting people in person versus online?

on guidance counselor 2.0, mark hinkle says the relationships he made in the analog world have made it possible to survive in the digital world. he says empathy and trust are built in person, not as easily on a screen. he thinks showing up to events and shaking hands matters more now because it is hard to tell who is real online.
