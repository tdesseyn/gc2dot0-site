advocating for yourself w/ amy from hack your hr

guest: amy, hr leader at a global company, hack your hr

  • frame tough conversations around what is reasonable versus what is realistic to keep the discussion productive
  • remember pushback on a raise is often self protection from your boss, not a personal rejection
  • before taking on more work, get paid for what you are already doing and audit your job description
  • lead with candor by talking to your boss the way you would talk to a respected friend, honestly and directly
  • when recruiting or interviewing someone, ask how they are doing in their job search before jumping into the role details

questions this episode answers

what does amy from hack your hr say about using reasonable versus realistic in salary conversations?

on guidance counselor 2.0, amy from hack your hr says to frame salary asks using reasonable versus realistic. she recommends acknowledging that while a raise may not be realistic right now due to budget constraints, you can still align with your boss on what is reasonable and fair based on your expanded responsibilities. this approach validates your worth even when the outcome is not immediate, and sets up future conversations about compensation.

how does amy from hack your hr suggest handling pushback when asking for a raise?

on guidance counselor 2.0, amy from hack your hr says to remember that pushback on a raise is usually not personal but self-protection from your manager. she explains that if your boss pushes back, they may be thinking they cannot get you a raise and need to manage the situation, not rejecting you personally. going into the conversation expecting this frame helps you regulate your emotions and stay focused on the facts.

what does amy from hack your hr recommend before taking on additional work?

on guidance counselor 2.0, amy from hack your hr recommends getting paid for what you are already doing before you take on more work. she suggests auditing your job description to ensure it accurately reflects your current responsibilities, then asking your manager to align on whether you are doing more than what you are paid to do. this prevents quiet promoting, where companies ask you to take on work above your level without adjusting your compensation.
