what peter bell covers in this episode

peter bell started as a developer, became a cto, and ran engineering at general assembly. he later ran the instructional team at flatiron school, co-founded the new york cto school, and created cto connection, a series of communities and conferences for engineering leaders. now he runs gather.dev, which he describes as building a knowledge graph for agentic engineering, where directors, vps and ctos share benchmarks and get answers from similar companies. he's also writing an o'reilly book on scaling ai adoption in engineering. this episode asks whether ai agents replace developers or make them far more productive, and what that means for getting hired.

proof of work is close to free

peter's headline point: the way you get a job now is by showing proof of work, and it's close to free. if you can find about 20 dollars a month, you can afford the tokens to show what you can do. taylor raises a developer who said he couldn't afford the pricier ai plans between jobs, and points to generous free tiers on some assistants. peter acknowledges life isn't fair. from his bootcamp years, he saw talented people who had elderly parents or family to care for and no spare time or money, unlike people whose college was paid for. but wherever you are is where you start.

more software, different roles

peter hopes the industry lands on jevons paradox: engineers can deliver so much more value that they're worth hiring, and lots of small companies will have a lot more software. he expects plenty of roles, including forward deployed engineers automating finance, accounting, customer service and operations.

he also sees engineering splitting into two broad paths. product engineers focus on delivering value to customers: finding the pain point, forming a hypothesis and running the simplest experiment to test demand. devx and tooling engineers build the harnesses, evaluators, loops and tooling that make product engineers faster. either way, he says, the job isn't writing code. it's solving problems.

your industry is your edge

taylor says industry used to matter less, because you could code for any industry. now deep knowledge of an industry like mortgages or healthcare, combined with ai, is a real advantage. peter agrees and adds that there aren't just good and bad developers. great developers can struggle in the wrong environment, so matching people to the right company, pace and problem space will matter more. his practical advice for standing out: pick a narrow niche at the intersection of two topics you care about, and instead of only applying online, build a small project for a specific hiring manager and reach out directly.

what makes a career durable

on long-term career durability, peter starts with agency. the robots can do almost anything, but only if you tell them to, so people who step up and say this is something i want to see in the world are what companies hire for. curiosity goes with it: experimenting to see what happens shows you'll push the envelope. next is learning faster than others, and community helps there, giving you trusted people to learn from. he remembers trading help with a friend years ago, fixing his sql queries while the friend fixed his jquery. he also warns developers not to rely only on assistants that do everything for them, because you get the output but little of the learning.
