how to tackle salary negotiations w/ john gates

guest: john gates, salary coach, former head of global recruiting for fortune 500 companies

  • when asked for salary expectations early on, answer with a range instead of a single number so you dont get locked in low
  • ask what the budget is for the position before giving your own number, then stay silent for several seconds after asking
  • if salary is capped, negotiate other things like signing bonus, title, level, incentive milestones, remote work, or even insurance premiums
  • position yourself as talking to other companies to create competition, since recruiters move faster and offer more when they fear losing you
  • even when an offer looks good take time to review it collaboratively before accepting, since there is often more left on the table than you realize

questions this episode answers

what does john gates say about when to give your salary expectations during the hiring process?

on guidance counselor 2.0, john gates says to ask for the company's budget before giving your own number, then use a range instead of a single figure. after asking what their budget is, stay silent for about eight seconds to create pressure for them to answer first. offering a range instead of a point prevents you from getting locked into a low number that becomes the final offer.

what does john gates recommend negotiating if the salary is capped?

on guidance counselor 2.0, john gates says to negotiate signing bonuses, title, level, milestone bonuses tied to specific projects, remote versus in-person arrangements, and sometimes even health insurance premiums. he mentions that signing bonuses in the five-figure range are often preapproved and just sitting there, and that changing your level (like from developer two to developer three) can unlock a different salary bracket entirely.

why does john gates say to mention other companies during salary negotiations?

on guidance counselor 2.0, john gates says mentioning competition with other companies invokes a psychology concept called mimetic desire, where people want what someone else has. recruiters move faster and offer more when they fear losing you to a competitor, and positioning yourself as in demand makes you appear more valuable, even if the other opportunities are not identical roles.
