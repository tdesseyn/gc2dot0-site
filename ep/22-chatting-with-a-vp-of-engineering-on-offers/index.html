ep 22 · feb 22, 2021

chatting with a vp of engineering on offers

guest: jack evans, vice president of engineering at evidence care

  • give a specific salary number instead of a vague range so hiring managers know where you actually stand
  • know your full compensation picture upfront, including benefits, bonus and 401k match, before entering negotiations
  • if a candidate asks for much more than expected, treat it as a signal to review your team's pay, not a red flag on the candidate
  • come prepared with research and clear expectations, since that preparation signals how you will perform on the job
  • once you accept an offer, don't renege if something better comes along, since that damages trust with the employer

questions this episode answers

what does jack evans say about candidates giving a salary range versus a specific number?

on guidance counselor 2.0, jack evans, vice president of engineering at evidence care, says candidates should give a specific number rather than a range. he explains that a range puts the first step back onto the hiring manager's shoulders, while the candidate knows themselves and where they fit in the market better than anyone else could. giving a specific number shows the candidate has done their homework and knows what they want.

what does jack evans recommend knowing about compensation before negotiating?

on guidance counselor 2.0, jack evans and taylor desseyn agree candidates should know their full compensation picture before entering negotiations. taylor says job seekers need to know how much they pay out of pocket for benefits, what their bonus looks like, and what their 401k match is on the front end. jack adds that at smaller companies the whole compensation package matters, including equity options, working schedule, and performance-based bonuses, not just base salary.

what does jack evans say about candidates who ask for much higher salaries than expected?

on guidance counselor 2.0, jack evans says when a candidate asks for significantly more than expected, he treats it as a signal that his team's compensation might need adjustment, not as a red flag on the candidate. for small startups, keeping the existing team together matters most because losing people causes major productivity hits, so he views high salary requests as market data points that help him ensure current employees are fairly valued and want to stay for two to three years.
