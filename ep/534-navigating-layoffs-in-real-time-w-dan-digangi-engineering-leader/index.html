navigating layoffs in real time w/ dan digangi, engineering leader

guest: dan digangi, engineering leader

what dan digangi covers in this episode

dan digangi has worked in engineering for more than twenty years, the last ten in engineering leadership, with stops at docusign and apartments.com. until about two and a half weeks before this episode, he ran all of engineering at postmark, a transactional email company, which he calls the coolest product and team he's worked on. it was his second layoff from the same company, and his earlier one was also covered on guidance counselor 2.0. this time he came on to talk through a layoff in real time: what he's doing differently, and how he's using ai in the search.

come out of the gate with momentum

the biggest change for dan this time is pushing much harder on his network, right away. after his first layoff he waited about three months because he was panicking, and the search stretched to six months. this time he's having as many direct conversations as he can, with people he knows and people one step removed. his linkedin and x posts about the layoff took off, and even if he ends up taking a few months off, the momentum gives him a reason to follow up with anyone he wants. he compares the networking muscle to going to the gym: it's a skill you build with reps.

find somebody before you cold apply

dan tells a story from his last search. he had a referral into figma, and the director told him it was a good thing, because he had 50 referrals and hadn't even gotten to the direct applies yet. dan's rule since: before you cold apply, always find somebody at the company. but he's just as clear that the sleaziest move is asking strangers to refer you. if someone doesn't know you, a dm asking to work where they work goes nowhere. build the relationship first.

direct applies still go on his list, but as a to-do, not the thing most likely to work. he says you don't need to be on every channel, but you should have a few different hooks out there. taylor's version: you can't build a house with a screwdriver.

ai as a job search assistant

dan has used chatgpt since it first showed up. in this search he handed it a pile of job links from greenhouse, lever and workday postings and had it build a spreadsheet: company, title, location, remote, and comp when it was listed. he gave it his resume, background and what he likes and doesn't like, and had it rate each role as a strong or medium match, with a dedicated remote column because remote is table stakes for him. for companies he might interview with, he also had it break down what they do from their website. taylor adds a caution: never let ai write your resume and skip reviewing it line by line.

dan also makes the case that paying for ai help during a search can be worth it if it gets you to an offer sooner, since every week without income costs something too.

keep a running record of your wins

the last theme is documenting your work. dan says he's always telling himself, and the people he manages, to keep track of wins so they're not scrambling to remember what they did. taylor pushes the same idea for job seekers: post small updates about what you've accomplished, not for likes, but because most people are bad at explaining their work when put on the spot.
