ep 261 · aug 18, 2023

scaling an engineering organization of 300+ developers

guest: ryan and yule, staff software engineer and director of engineering at volvo cars

  • use a rotating roster for technical assessments instead of asking for volunteers so reviews don't stall out
  • give take-home assignments a time cap and ask candidates to prioritize what they solve so you see judgment not just free time
  • submit interviewer feedback separately before comparing notes so the panel doesn't bias each other
  • have recruiters lead candidate-to-team matching once they know the tech space well enough to spot the right fit
  • set expectations early that candidates may not know their exact team until later in a fast-growing org

who ryan and yule are

ryan is a staff software engineer at volvo cars, working with three teams on pricing: price calculations, taxes and discounts, so his systems sit behind almost any volvo price you see online or at a dealership. yule is director of engineering for the same area, after years at tech startups in gothenburg, sweden. volvo is moving from selling cars wholesale to a direct sales model, with a goal of being fully direct by 2030, and their part of the company, about 1,000 people across engineering and product, grew 20 to 25 percent in a year, more than 300 developers. this episode is a behind the scenes look at how they hired that fast without letting quality or fairness slip.

key takeaways

1. one-at-a-time hiring doesn't scale

before the push, hiring meant finding one person for one team. that didn't work at this volume. yule says the recruiters were key from day one, and the first bottleneck was the sheer number of applicants. engineers had to help recruiters decide where to set the bar and how to screen well. they also moved the take-home ahead of a hiring manager interview, because that interview had become a bottleneck, and they tried to cut the number of meetings each candidate needed.

2. bottom up or top down: they let each director choose

a listener asked whether they hired leaders first or built the teams first. there was no org-wide rule. yule went bottom up, since most of his teams existed, and he didn't want to lose speed. he ended up with 50 people and no engineering managers besides himself, and in hindsight says he probably should have done a bit of both at once.

3. a rotating roster fixed a two week delay

assessments used to go to a pool of engineers in one slack channel, and nobody raised their hand. reviews took about two weeks, and they lost strong applicants for being slow. the recruiters' fix: put every reviewer on a roster, assign the next name, give them 48 hours, and let them pass if they're out or traveling. ryan says most assessments now come back within 48 hours of submission.

4. cap the take-home and grade the tradeoffs

taylor's view is to meet candidates where they are, since some people have kids and no time for a take-home. ryan agrees the bias is real: take-homes favor people with a stable home life and spare time. so they cap it at four hours and include more work than fits, so candidates must prioritize. what you leave undone is part of the evaluation. the problem is a toll calculation for a car moving through a city, close to the team's real work.

5. two interviewers, separate feedback, a script

after the take-home, ryan leads a technical interview with pair programming and system design questions while a second engineer shadows. both submit feedback separately so neither biases the other. the culture interview works the same way: one lead, one shadow, on a rotation, so yule isn't interviewing candidates for his own team at that stage. a script keeps it standardized but flexible, they spend at least five minutes setting expectations, and leave time for questions. instead of a one to four score, they write a summary with a clear proceed or don't proceed recommendation and areas to dig into.

6. interview for the org, then match the role

because they're filling so many roles, ryan rarely rejects at the technical stage. he places candidates by level and possible teams so later interviewers can decide well. candidates usually don't know their team up front, and they're told so from the start. after the culture interview, recruiters match them to a role and the hiring manager runs a more casual interview to sell it. an early hiring manager panel did the matching, until the recruiters got good enough that it wasn't needed.

7. be honest about what's still broken

ryan's open problem is consistency in technical interviews, since each rotating interviewer approaches it differently, and rubrics are harder for junior and mid-level candidates whose skills vary widely. yule's is that teams feel left in the dark: sometimes a new person just shows up. retention has been good, which they credit to big problems to solve and honesty that a team growing 20 percent may look different by your first day. hiring juniors in that much change is still one of their hardest challenges.

what to do this week

  • if you hire, find where candidates wait longest in your process and fix that one step first.
  • put assessment reviewers on a named rotation with a 48 hour turnaround.
  • if you use a take-home, cap the time and ask candidates to explain what they chose not to finish.
  • have interviewers submit written feedback separately before discussing a candidate.
  • if you're a candidate in a process like this, ask on the first call whether you're interviewing for a specific team.
