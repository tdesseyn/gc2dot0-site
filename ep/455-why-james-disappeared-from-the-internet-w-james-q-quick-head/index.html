why james disappeared from the internet w/ james q quick, head of dev exp

guest: james q quick, head of developer experience at makeswift (bigcommerce)

what james q quick covers in this episode

james q quick is head of developer experience at makeswift, part of bigcommerce, and one of the better known developer content creators, with a big run on youtube. he'd been a frequent guest on guidance counselor 2.0, and this time he came on to explain why he went quiet online: burnout from full-time content creation, the move back to a full-time role, and how that job started with a conversation at a conference.

burnout when it's your full-time job

james is transparent that money wasn't the problem. he made good money creating content. the pressure was that every day it was his responsibility to make sure the income kept coming. when his first child was born, there wasn't really parental leave. he took about two weeks before getting back to work. with his new role, he'll have actual parental leave for baby number two.

he says he didn't realize how burnt out he was. after one year of big success on youtube, the numbers dropped, and he tried to get more creative in ways that weren't natural to him, which he thinks showed. creating content felt different once it was his only job. his advice to anyone romanticizing freelancing: you do get to do what you want, but there's a lot of pressure.

a calculated risk, not a leap

james wants people to know he didn't jump off a cliff. he only went full time because he was already making significant income on the side, and he gave himself about six months of runway, knowing he could find a job if it failed. he and his family are very fiscally responsible, with no debt, and he says he doesn't want to encourage anyone to take a big financial risk. he also didn't want to keep going if it only covered the basics.

networking is just showing up

asked how to approach a company's booth at a conference without being salesy, james shares one of his strongest takes: he hates the word networking. it sounds cold, like a firm handshake and a pitch. to him, networking is investing in your community and being part of the conversation. his makeswift conversation didn't start at the booth. it started in a lunch line, talking about react, next.js and astro with two people he didn't know yet.

he's honest that you can't promise talking to people leads to a job. but the more people you know, the better your chances when an opportunity opens up. listen for what skills companies are looking for, even at companies you won't join, and go build those. he says content was a big advantage for him, and that engaging, commenting, posting and asking questions makes a real difference compared to someone who never does.

making conferences less intimidating

his biggest conference tip: bring a posse, a phrase he credits to aaron francis. one or two people you trust to sit down with makes meeting others much easier. if you don't know anyone, find people posting about the event online, comment, and ask for coffee. accept that many people cluster with people they already know, and sit at a random table anyway. to save money, volunteer. many conferences give you a free ticket for working part of the day, so you can still catch talks and evening events.

questions this episode answers

what does james q quick say about burnout from full-time content creation?

on guidance counselor 2.0, james q quick says he burned out from full-time content creation even though he controlled his schedule and made good money. the constant pressure to earn a living and be responsible for every aspect of creating, marketing, and producing content took a toll he did not initially realize. he found the stress of maintaining income and visibility while numbers dropped mentally exhausting, and the burnout only became clear after starting a full-time job with parental leave and set boundaries.

what does james q quick say about networking at conferences?

on guidance counselor 2.0, james q quick says networking is not about cold introductions at booths with firm handshakes but about showing up and investing in your community. he advises bringing a friend or two to make sitting at random tables less intimidating, volunteering at conferences to get free or discounted tickets while still attending talks, and accepting that almost everyone feels uncomfortable so you should not let awkwardness hold you back from starting conversations.
